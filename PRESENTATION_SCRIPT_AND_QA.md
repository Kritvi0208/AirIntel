## Part 2: Verbatim Oral Delivery Script

### Slide 1: Title (0:00 – 1:00)
> *"Good morning, respected professors and fellow batchmates. Today, I present an inquiry into one of the most critical constitutional and media policy dilemmas of our time: **Topic 35 — President’s Rule under Article 356 and the 2025 Manipur Imposition**.*  
> *Public policy in modern India is rarely formulated in an ivory tower. It is amplified, delayed, shaped, and often distorted by the fourth estate. This presentation examines that reciprocal dynamic: how the press covered the crisis, how coverage influenced central policy intervention, and how state instruments—from internet shutdowns to criminal defamation—reshaped the ground rules of journalism itself."*

### Slide 2: The Instrument — Article 356 (1:00 – 2:15)
> *"Before we examine media coverage, we must anchor our discussion strictly in the constitutional instrument. Article 356 empowers the President to assume the powers of a State Government and transfer legislative authority to Parliament when the constitutional machinery has broken down.*  
> *Under the landmark 1994 Supreme Court ruling in **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India**, this power is neither unfettered nor purely subjective. It requires objective material demonstrating that governance has become impossible. Article 356 follows a four-step lifecycle: Trigger, Proclamation, Parliamentary Approval within two months, and Extension in six-month tranches up to three years. Notably, Manipur has experienced President’s Rule eleven times since 1956—the highest of any state in the Indian Union."*

### Slide 3: Chronological Timeline (2:15 – 3:30)
> *"Let us review the empirical sequence of events. On 3 May 2023, ethnic violence erupted between the Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities following a High Court directive on tribal status. The ensuing conflict claimed over 260 lives and displaced 60,000 citizens.*  
> *Yet Article 356 was not imposed in 2023, nor in 2024. It was proclaimed on **13 February 2025**, four days after Chief Minister N. Biren Singh resigned amid internal BJP factional deadlock. Parliament ratified the proclamation in April 2025 and granted a six-month extension in August 2025, before central rule was finally revoked on **4 February 2026** when a new council of ministers was sworn in."*

### Slide 4: Two Ways to Read "Why Now" (3:30 – 4:45)
> *"This chronology presents a fundamental public policy puzzle: **Why was Article 356 imposed in February 2025, after 21 months of conflict?***  
> *In the public sphere, two competing frames emerged:  
> 1. **The Political Trigger:** The administrative consensus within the ruling party had collapsed. Thirty-two MLAs could not agree on leadership, and a no-confidence motion was imminent. Central rule was enacted to avoid floor defeat.  
> 2. **The Humanitarian Reading:** Policy analysts like Dr. Thongkholal Haokip argued the state had been 'politically unattended' during its worst bloodshed. The Centre acted only when political arithmetic demanded it, not when humanitarian devastation was at its peak. Which frame the public received depended entirely on the media outlet they consumed."*

### Slides 5 to 8: Four Empirical Case Studies (4:45 – 10:15)
> *"Let us examine four concrete, dated case studies of media coverage:*  
> * **Case 1 (May–July 2023): TV Primetime's Pre-Viral Silence.** For over 75 days, national television largely ignored the violence. Prime-time real estate was dominated by diplomatic state visits and local elections. It was only on July 19, 2023, when graphic footage of two women paraded naked went viral, that primetime exploded into outrage, forcing the Prime Minister's first public remarks. As *Newslaundry* observed, coverage followed viral shock value, not sustained public interest reporting.*  
> * **Case 2 (13 Feb 2025): Reporting the Imposition.** The proclamation coverage was sharply partisan. *The Wire* headlined it as a 'belated admission of failure' after 652 days, quoting Jairam Ramesh. Conversely, pro-government networks framed it as proactive crisis management. Crucially, regional reports documented that Kuki-Zo BJP MLAs welcomed President’s Rule as preferable to an elected Meitei-led state executive.*  
> * **Case 3 (April & August 2025): Parliamentary Debates.** During the statutory resolution and extension debates, coverage moved from the conflict zones into Parliament. Home Minister Amit Shah's claim of 'zero violence since November 2024' was broadcast verbatim, contrasted with opposition walkouts. Yet mainstream media failed to independently verify official peace claims in the relief camps—parliamentary theatre replaced investigative journalism.*  
> * **Case 4 (Sep–Dec 2023): Policy Turning on the Press.** When the Editors Guild of India released an objective fact-finding report citing one-sided local coverage, Manipur police registered criminal FIRs against its editors under IPC Sections 153A and 505. The Supreme Court stayed coercive action, protecting press scrutiny. Simultaneously, Manipur suffered over 5,000 hours of internet blackouts—the longest globally in 2023—crippling verification until struck down by the Manipur High Court on 1 December 2023."*

### Slide 9: Perspective Mapping (10:15 – 11:45)
> *"To ensure academic evenhandedness, Slide 9 maps five distinct vantage points:  
> 1. **Union Government:** Framed Article 356 as neutral, corrective stabilization to restore order.  
> 2. **Opposition:** Denounced the 21-month delay as unconstitutional neglect, framing the move as saving party face.  
> 3. **Civil Society:** Deeply split along ethnic lines—Kuki groups welcomed central rule over state police, while Meitei civil society protested the suspension of popular representation.  
> 4. **Judiciary:** The Supreme Court cited an 'absolute breakdown of constitutional machinery' and safeguarded journalist freedoms.  
> 5. **Media Institutions:** Divided between national press bodies defending critical investigation and local press unions rejecting external critique."*

### Slides 10 & 11: Critical Analysis — The Two-Way Dynamic (11:45 – 14:30)
> *"How did media influence policy, and how did policy influence media?  
> First, **coverage shaped policy through an agenda-setting lag.** Because media coverage was sporadic and viral-dependent, national political pressure took 21 months to build. However, once attention crossed a critical threshold, the Centre could no longer sustain a hands-off approach when the state cabinet imploded.  
> Second, **the policy environment transformed media freedoms.** The weaponization of FIRs, combined with 5,000 hours of digital shutdowns, created a pervasive chilling effect. Journalists were caught between armed civil groups and state prosecution, leading to stenographic journalism where press releases replaced investigation."*

### Slide 12: Comparative Dimensions (14:30 – 15:45)
> *"A comparative lens highlights the stark contrast between vernacular and national media:  
> * **Local & Vernacular Press** had superior geographic proximity and source access, but became deeply polarized along ethnic lines—valley newspapers adopted Meitei narratives, while hill portals adopted Kuki-Zo frames under duress from local armed groups.  
> * **National English & Hindi Media** possessed institutional reach and constitutional authority, but practiced 'helicopter journalism'—visiting only during sensational moments and abandoning the state during the crucial recovery phases."*

### Slides 13 & 14: Critical Evaluation & Conclusion (15:45 – 18:00)
> *"Where does media performance lie on the evaluative spectrum?  
> On our evaluative scale, coverage was **Sensational** on primetime TV, **Captured** in partisan regional outlets, and **Mixed** among digital investigative portals. Most critically, it was **Severely Deficient as a Deliberative Space**.  
> **In conclusion, and as my explicit evaluative position:**  
> Media coverage did not cause the imposition of Article 356—that was driven by intra-party arithmetic. But media's upstream failure of sustained explanatory journalism allowed the crisis to fester unattended for 21 months, while its downstream stenography normalized the prolonged suspension of democratic governance. Manipur 2025 demonstrates the acute danger when the Fourth Estate responds only to algorithms of outrage rather than the steady imperatives of constitutional accountability. Thank you."*

## Part 3: Anticipated Questions & Expert Q&A Defense

### Q1: "Did the media truly cause any delay in policy, or was the Union Government's delay purely strategic?"
**Defense Answer:**
> *"The delay was undoubtedly driven by political and strategic calculus at the Centre. However, public policy theory—specifically John Kingdon’s Multiple Streams Framework—shows that policy windows open when problems, policies, and politics align. For 75 days, the 'problem stream' was suppressed because national media maintained a near-total blackout. When the July 2023 viral video broke the media threshold, the political cost of total silence became untenable, forcing judicial intervention and parliamentary debate. The media did not create the delay alone, but its algorithmic gatekeeping provided political cover for executive inaction."*

### Q2: "Isn't an internet shutdown justifiable during severe ethnic rioting to prevent hate speech?"
**Defense Answer:**
> *"While temporary restrictions under the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services Rules, 2017 are legally permissible to prevent incitement, proportionality is the constitutional test established in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020). The Manipur High Court’s landmark ruling on 1 December 2023 explicitly held that a blanket, 5,000-hour shutdown was disproportionate and counterproductive. Instead of quelling rumors, the information vacuum allowed unverified disinformation to spread via offline word-of-mouth, while preventing citizens from accessing banking, relief aid, and emergency medical services."*

### Q3: "Why did the Editors Guild of India face local backlash if their report was objective?"
**Defense Answer:**
> *"The conflict between the Editors Guild of India and local bodies like the All Manipur Working Journalists' Union (AMWJU) illustrates the sociological fracture of war reporting. Local journalists operated under immediate, physical existential threats from armed vigilante groups in both the valley and the hills. When the national Editors Guild conducted a rapid four-day fact-finding mission and concluded that local media was one-sided, local press bodies felt their physical vulnerability and complex operational reality were dismissed from an elite Delhi vantage point. It highlights the profound divide between detached national watchdog bodies and vulnerable local frontline journalists."*

### Q4: "Was President's Rule in Manipur in 2025 legally compliant with the S.R. Bommai doctrine?"
**Defense Answer:**
> *"Under S.R. Bommai, Article 356 cannot be invoked for mere political instability or internal party quarrels if an alternative government can be explored. In February 2025, the Assembly was placed in 'suspended animation' rather than dissolved. The constitutional rationale offered was the breakdown of law and order alongside executive paralysis. However, critics argue that the Assembly was suspended specifically to preserve the BJP legislature's ability to regroup without facing elections—a practice that, while legally tolerated under Bommai, tests the spirit of federal autonomy."*
